The answer isn't to compete with AI or hand the whole design process over to it. A screen can look polished and still solve the wrong problem. A beautifully written answer can still be based on bad information. And a generated interface can still make absolutely no sense for the person using it.

That's because design has never really been about making screens. It's about figuring out what the problem actually is, understanding who you're solving it for, making decisions, spotting what's wrong, and knowing when an idea needs to change. AI can make parts of that process much faster. But knowing where it actually belongs in your workflow is the interesting part.

So instead of asking, “What can AI design for me?”, let's look at a better question: How can AI make the way I design better?

## How to Get Started With AI in Design

So, where do you actually start? If you've looked at the AI landscape lately, you already know the problem. There are tools for research, tools for images, tools for UI, tools for code, tools for writing, and another tool appearing every time you open your browser. It's very easy to start collecting tools without actually changing how you work.

That's why I'd start somewhere else: start with the task, not the tool. Think about what you're actually doing. Maybe you're entering an industry you know nothing about. Maybe you're trying to understand competitors. Maybe you're stuck on a user flow. Maybe the landing page copy isn't working. Maybe you have an idea in your head but don't know what the interface should look like yet. Those are much better starting points than “Which AI tool should I try?” Once you know the problem, you can figure out whether AI is useful for it.

**Research & Discovery:** Research is usually one of the first places AI becomes useful. Not because it replaces research, but because getting oriented can take a surprisingly long time. Imagine you're designing a product for an industry you've never worked in before. You could spend the next few hours opening dozens of articles, comparing terminology, looking at competitors, and trying to piece everything together. Or you can use AI to get a rough map of the territory first. Ask it to explain the industry. Ask what the common products are. Ask about the terminology. Ask it to compare competitors or point out patterns worth investigating. Then go deeper yourself. That's an important distinction. AI is great at helping you get from “I know nothing about this” to “Okay, I understand what I need to investigate.” But don't treat the first answer as your research. Use it to find the trail. Then follow the trail yourself.

**Ideation & Problem Solving:** Then there's the blank-canvas problem. You know what you're trying to solve, but your brain has decided that today is a great day to produce absolutely nothing.

![UI kit](/blog/robot.gif)

This is where AI can be a surprisingly useful thinking partner. You can ask for different approaches to a feature, alternative user flows, possible edge cases, or even deliberately bad solutions. And honestly, the bad ideas can be useful too.

Sometimes you don't need AI to give you the answer. You just need something to react to. You see an idea and think, “No, that's not right because…” And suddenly you know what you actually want. That's a much more interesting way to think about AI and creativity. You're not asking it to be creative instead of you. You're giving yourself something to push against.

**Copy & Content:** Designers write a lot. Button labels. Empty states. Error messages. Headings. Onboarding screens. Feature descriptions. Landing pages. Tiny pieces of copy that somehow take twenty minutes to get right. AI is pretty good at helping with the first pass. Give it a paragraph and ask it to make it shorter. Give it a headline and ask for ten alternatives. Try a friendlier tone. Remove the jargon. Make the explanation clearer. The useful part isn't that it can produce ten headlines in three seconds. It's that you no longer have to stare at the same sentence for ten minutes waiting for a better version to appear. You can generate options quickly, then use your own judgment to choose what actually sounds right.

**Visual Design:** This is probably where most people think of AI first. Generate an illustration. Make a moodboard. Explore a visual direction. Create some marketing imagery. Try a completely different style. And yes, that's useful. But there's a difference between generating a visual and designing a visual system. AI can give you fifty interesting images. That doesn't mean any of them belong in your product. The designer still has to decide whether the image fits the brand, whether it communicates the right thing, whether it works with the rest of the interface, and whether it actually helps the user. Sometimes the best use of generated visuals isn't even the final asset. It might simply be a quick way to see whether a direction is worth exploring.

**Design Review & Feedback:** AI can also be useful when you've already made something and want another pair of eyes on it. Not because it can tell you whether your design is “good,” but because it can help you notice things you might have stopped seeing after staring at the same screen for too long. You can give it a screenshot, a user flow, or even describe the problem you're solving and ask it to question your decisions. Is the hierarchy clear? Is there anything confusing? What happens if the user doesn't follow the expected path? Are there edge cases you haven't considered? Sometimes you won't agree with what it suggests, and that's completely fine. The point isn't to accept every piece of feedback. It's to have something that makes you stop and look at your design from another angle. Used this way, AI becomes less of a design generator and more of a second set of eyes. You still make the final call, but you don't have to rely on your own perspective alone.

**Prototyping & Validation:** This is where things get really interesting. An idea doesn't have to stay trapped in a Figma file until the end of the project anymore. You can describe an interaction, generate a rough interface, add sample content, and get something people can actually click through surprisingly quickly. And that changes the conversation. Instead of asking: “Do you think this idea would work?” You can sometimes ask: “Here, try it.”
That means you can discover problems much earlier. Maybe the flow is confusing. Maybe the feature doesn't feel useful. Maybe the layout falls apart with real content. Finding that out early is much cheaper than finding it out after you've spent days polishing the idea.

## Choosing the Right AI Tool for the Stage You're In

Once you know what you're trying to do, choosing an AI tool becomes a little easier. You don't need the same tool for every stage. The right one depends on how far along you are and how much control you need.

**When You Have No Idea Yet** <br/>
When you're starting with a blank page, a general-purpose AI chatbot tool can be enough. ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini can help you throw ideas around, understand a problem, explore different directions, or figure out what you should be thinking about before you start designing. At this stage, you don't need AI to build anything. You just need something to help you get unstuck.

**When You Have an Idea and Want to See It**<br/>
Once you have a direction, you can move from talking about the idea to actually seeing it on a screen. v0 and Lovable are useful here if you want to quickly generate a website or interface without dealing with much technical setup. They're a good fit for designers who want to turn an idea into a working prototype and make changes through prompts. There are some limitations once you want to move beyond a simple prototype. v0 uses shadcn by default, and using your own custom design system requires setting up a registry. Lovable also doesn't let you directly import and use Radian components, so it's more suitable when you're exploring a standalone website rather than working with an existing component system.

![Component ](/blog/v0.png)

**When You Want More Control** <br/>
If you want more control over how your prototype is built, Replit gives you more flexibility. You can choose the framework and tech stack you want to work with instead of being limited to one setup. It also gives you templates and examples to start from, which can be useful when you want to move beyond a quick prototype and have more control over how the project is put together.

**When You Want to Work Inside Your Design System**<br/>
This is where Antigravity becomes useful. Instead of starting with a blank project and letting AI create everything from scratch, you can work with your existing design system and use its components when generating an interface. It's also useful when you're iterating on an existing design. You can make a change, see how it affects the interface, and keep refining it without rebuilding everything from the beginning. If this is the stage you're at, there's one thing you need to do before you start: set up your design system inside the code editor. And luckily, you don't have to make that process complicated.

## How to Easily Setup your Design System in the AI code editor

So you've reached the point where you want AI to work with the system you've already designed, rather than inventing everything from scratch. Your design system already contains decisions about components, styles, patterns, and tokens. Giving AI access to those decisions helps keep the generated interface closer to your product instead of falling into the same generic patterns you see everywhere. And setting it up is simpler than it sounds.

**1. Start With a Folder:** Before asking AI to build anything, create a folder for the project. This gives you a place to set everything up and gives AI a proper project to work inside, rather than treating your design as a one-off mockup.

**2. Install Your Design System:** Now, head to the installation page of the design system you're using and copy the installation command. Open your project in your code editor, paste the command into the terminal, and run it. That's it. Once the installation finishes, the design system and its components are ready to use in your project.

**3. Give It References:** Now tell AI what you're trying to build. A screenshot can show the visual direction. A URL can show how an existing experience works. Your prompt can explain the goal, content, and constraints. For example, instead of “Build a pricing page.” You could provide a screenshot of a pricing layout you like, a URL to an existing product for reference, and explain what your own pricing page needs to accomplish. Suddenly, AI has much less room to make things up.

**4. Generate, Break, Fix, Repeat:** The first version might still be wrong. The preview might break. A component might not behave correctly. The layout might need changes. That's normal. One useful part of working with AI in code is that you can often take the error, give it back to the AI, and let it try to fix the problem. Then you review the result again. And again. And again. The point isn't to get a perfect interface from one prompt. The point is that the distance between “I have an idea” and “I have something I can test” is getting much shorter.

## Getting Better Results With AI

The first prompt is rarely the interesting one. You ask for something, get a generic result, and think the tool isn't very good. Sometimes the problem isn't the tool. It simply doesn't know enough yet. And no you do not have to master prompt engineering. Compare: “Design a pricing page.” with: “Design a pricing page for a project management tool used by small creative teams. The main goal is to make the difference between the three plans easy to understand. Keep the layout simple, make the middle plan the most prominent, and use short, friendly copy.”

The second prompt gives AI somewhere to go. You're not making the AI smarter. You're removing some of the guessing. That's where most people start, but better prompts are only half the story. The other half is the process itself. The way you structure the conversation, provide context, and work through a problem with AI can have just as much impact on the result as the prompt you start with.

## Different Types of Prompts

Not all prompts serve the same purpose. What you ask AI for can change quite a bit depending on where you are in the project. Sometimes you're still throwing ideas around, sometimes you know exactly what you want, and sometimes showing AI something is much easier than trying to explain it. Knowing what kind of input to give can make a big difference in what you get back.

**Exploratory prompts** are useful when you're still figuring things out. You can ask AI to come up with feature ideas, suggest different user flows, point out potential problems, or explore different ways of solving the same problem. You don't necessarily need a final answer at this stage. You're using AI to open up the problem, see what's possible, and find a few directions you can take further.

**Detailed prompts** become more useful once you have a clearer idea of what you're trying to build. A prompt like “generate a landing page” doesn't give AI much to work with, so you're likely to get something fairly generic. Add details about the audience, product, goals, tone of voice, content, functionality, and any constraints you're working with, and the result can become much more relevant. The more context you give it, the less AI has to guess.

**Image-based prompts** can be especially useful when you're working on interfaces. A screenshot, wireframe, mockup, or even a rough sketch can communicate things that are difficult to explain through text alone. AI can see the layout, hierarchy, spacing, component relationships, visual style, and overall direction without you having to describe every little detail.

**URL-based prompts** are useful when you're working with an existing product or website. Giving AI a URL can provide context about the actual content, structure, functionality, and user flow. This can come in handy when you're researching competitors, reviewing an existing experience, auditing content, or simply trying to understand how another product has approached a similar problem.

But here's the thing: not every reference gives AI the same amount of useful information. A text-only prompt can leave plenty of room for interpretation, which can lead to broader or more generic results. A URL gives AI more context about the product and how it works, while a screenshot can make the visual direction much clearer. And you don't have to pick just one. A screenshot can show AI what you want it to look like, a URL can give it more context about the product, and your prompt can explain what you're actually trying to achieve. Put those together, and AI has a much clearer picture of what you're working with.

## Stop Treating Every Prompt Like a Command

There's another change that makes a big difference: Don't always start by asking AI to make something. If you're working on a complicated problem, give it the context first. You can literally say: “Don't build anything yet. Just show me that you understand the full picture.” That's it. Now you can see what AI thinks the problem is before it starts solving it. Maybe it misunderstood the audience. Maybe it missed an important constraint. Maybe it made an assumption you never intended. It's much easier to fix that at the beginning than after you've generated an entire interface.

From there, the conversation can look more like an actual design process:
-Give AI the context.
-Ask it to summarize what it understood.
-Correct anything it got wrong.
-Work through open questions.
-Ask for alternatives.
-Challenge ideas that don't make sense.
-Make your decisions.
-Then ask it to generate something.

That's a lot closer to how designers already work. The difference is that now you have another participant to bounce ideas off. And just like design itself, the good stuff usually appears after a few rounds of iteration rather than in the first attempt.

## From Design-First to AI-Assisted Development

For a long time, the process was pretty straightforward: Designer designs. Developer develops. The work gets handed over. Then everyone discovers the things that didn't quite survive the handoff. AI is starting to blur that line. A designer can explore an idea and turn it into something interactive much earlier. A developer can use AI to generate and experiment with interfaces without waiting for every detail to be finalized. Teams can try things, break things, test things, and change direction sooner.

That doesn't make the designer less important. If anything, it puts more attention on the parts of design that were always difficult to automate: understanding the problem, knowing the user, asking the right questions, spotting a bad solution, and making the final call. AI can generate a lot of things. It can't decide whether the thing it generated is actually worth building. That's still our job.

## When Not to Use AI

For all the excitement around AI, there are also plenty of times when you should simply… not use it. If it takes you longer to explain the task, review the output, fix the mistakes, and rewrite everything than it would have taken to do the job yourself, what's the point? There are also things you shouldn't blindly hand over to AI. Important research findings need verification. Sensitive product information needs care. Major design decisions still need people who understand the users, the business, and the context. And sometimes the fastest workflow is still the old one. That's okay. Using AI doesn't mean putting AI into every task just because you can.

## Conclusion

AI is changing the way designers work. But maybe the most interesting change isn't that AI can now generate a screen in a few seconds. It's that the distance between an idea and something you can actually explore is getting smaller. You can research faster. Get unstuck faster. Try more directions. Test an idea before investing days into it. Move from a conversation to a prototype without everything having to happen in separate stages. The trick is not to let AI take over the process. It's to bring it into the process at the right moments. Use it when you're stuck. Use it when you need another direction. Use it when a repetitive task is slowing you down. Give it context. Question what it gives you. Keep iterating. And when it gives you something that doesn't make sense? Don't be afraid to say: “Nope. Let's try that again.”
